An old philosophical dilemma: how can *individuality* exist if everything in the universe is connected? Or the other way round: where does *connectedness* come from if every object is unique? It is usually staged as a paradox. At the technical level it is simply *two characteristics of one system*.

Here is how it breaks down.

## Integrated information theory: two numbers

Back in 1998 Giulio Tononi and Gerald Edelman described consciousness through two properties, integration and differentiation. Tononi later folded them into a single measure, Φ (phi), integrated information, but for our question it helps to pull them apart again.

The first number is integration, which I will write as Φ. It shows *how much* a system behaves as a single whole.

The second is the *diversity of states* the system can take. Call it D. It shows *how many* distinguishable configurations the system can sustain.

These are different quantities, and both are needed.

High Φ with low D gives a monolith like a crystal: unified but monotonous, the same state repeated throughout.

High D with low Φ gives a heap of sand: many parts, each sitting in its own state with no link to its neighbours; varied, but formless.

Consciousness lives where both numbers are high: there are many states, and all of them are bound into one experience.

## What this means for unity and multiplicity

The two characteristics complement each other: unity is measured through Φ, multiplicity through D.

A human being is a system with fairly high Φ (the world is experienced as a whole) and very high D (thousands of distinguishable thoughts, feelings and perceptions a day).

A beehive, if you follow the theory's logic, is more integrated than a single bee and less integrated than a human brain, while the diversity of its states is enormous: thousands of bees, each in its own state. Nobody can measure this yet.

The Earth as a living biosphere may sit higher still on both axes. We have no precise figures: measuring Φ for such systems would take instruments that do not exist yet.

## Where this gets tested

In small systems Φ can be estimated approximately; Tononi, Boly, Massimini and Koch review these attempts (*Nature Reviews Neuroscience*, 2016). A derived measure, the perturbational complexity index (PCI), is already used in clinics to assess the level of consciousness in unresponsive patients (Casali et al., *Science Translational Medicine*, 2013). PCI tells wakefulness apart from sleep, anaesthesia and the vegetative state.

So the quantity can be estimated in practice, if only approximately. Exact computation is impossible; the approximation works.

In this model the philosophical dilemma of "unity or multiplicity" becomes an engineering question: what are this system's Φ and D? Each system occupies its own place on the (Φ, D) plane, and the argument ends there.

## What remains open

Why is integration accompanied by experience from the inside? That is David Chalmers's *hard problem of consciousness*. The theory records the link between Φ and consciousness, but it does not explain *why* high integration *feels* like anything.

Chalmers does not say there is no answer. He says that no amount of data about Φ will answer the question of why high Φ is like something from the inside. The question stays open, and that is where the edge of our knowledge runs.

## Practical takeaway

When someone tells you "everything is one, everything is connected", ask them to be specific: in what sense, and with what Φ? High Φ means a unified system in which every part carries information about the whole. Low Φ with high D means a collection whose connections are more imagined than real.

This is graph theory applied to connectivity, and it needs no mysticism.

Unity and multiplicity are two coordinates. The only question is *where on that plane* we happen to be.
